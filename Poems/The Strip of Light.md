# The Strip of Light

Eight by ten. I measured it. Of course I did.
You give an engineer a room, he'll pace it,
because the master's degree does not get confiscated
along with the belt and the shoelaces and the future.

Six items. Bed. Mattress. Shelf. Toilet. Sink. Window.
A child could catalog it. Several, probably, have.
The mattress met the definition of a mattress
the way a puddle meets the definition of a lake.

Ninety days. Then six months. Do the math.
I did the math. The math was the only thing in there
that didn't have latitude, didn't have a mood,
didn't review my file and shake its head.
One hundred eighty days. Four thousand three hundred
twenty hours. The math was clean. The math was kind.
The math was the only guard who never lied.

And there was the light.

A window near the ceiling, narrow as an apology,
and through it one strip of day, laid across the wall
at an angle that moved. That was the whole show.
That was the only thing in the room that moved.
I watched it like television. I watched it like a wife.
It came in high and slid down slow and left,
and I thought: there goes a day I'm not in.
There goes a whole city being a city without me.

You want to know what freedom is worth? Ask a man
who watched a stripe of sun cross cinderblock
for a hundred and eighty consecutive matinees
and never once got tired of the plot.

The lock made a sound. Not loud. Precise.
A bolt in a housing. Metal meeting metal.
The sound of a system doing what it's for,
and what it's for was me.

Here's the humor, since you asked for humor:
I'd spent my whole life in motion. Every direction
anyone would give me, I took. And then they gave me
a room with no directions in it, and a strip of light
that went one way, slowly, every day,
and I finally learned to sit still
by having it legally required.
