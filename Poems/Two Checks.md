# Two Checks

Lincoln Park. A restaurant too nice for students
and not nice enough for anyone else.
Bread basket. Water glasses. Steak, and whatever
the older one ordered, ordered twice.
I took out the checkbook. I wrote two checks.
I slid them across the table between the bread and the water
like a man passing a note in a class he'd already passed.

*I can get a job,* the older one said.
I said something. I don't remember what.
Something about architecture. Something about family
being a building and somebody has to hold up the roof
and the somebody is always the somebody,
and the somebody was me,
and I liked it. God help me, I liked it.
I liked being the wall.

Nine and eight. That's how old they were
when I walked up the driveway with the news about her
and they stopped playing and ran over
and looked up at me with faces that did not yet know
what a face does when it hears that.
I told them. I was fourteen.
I have been telling them things ever since.

I paid for the buildings they walked into.
I paid for the years they spent becoming men.
I dropped out of the one thing I wanted
because somebody had to hold it together,
and the somebody, see above.
I did not keep a ledger. I want that clear.
I did not keep a ledger,
which is why I can't tell you the exact amount
of what a man is owed for being the wall.
Nothing, it turns out. The wall is owed nothing.
The wall is a wall. You lean on it and go inside.

And then the roof came down on me instead.
And I looked around the rubble for the two boys
whose tuition I wrote between the bread and the water.
And they were not in the rubble.
They were not in the doorway.
They were not on the phone.
They were somewhere fine. Loyola was fine. DePaul was fine.
Everything about them, it was generally understood, was fine.

I don't know what I expected. A shovel, maybe.
A hand. A call. A sentence with my name in it
said to a third party in a tone that wasn't an apology.
I got the seven-foot pole.
I got the specific silence of men
who know exactly what they'd have to say
and have run the math on saying it
and found the math unfavorable.

They ran the math.
I taught them the math.
That's the part that sits in the lower back,
where the body holds what the mind won't carry.
I taught them to read a room, to count the cost,
to be the somebody. And they read the room,
and they counted the cost, and they decided
the somebody was not going to be them.

Here's the humor. You asked for humor.
I wrote those checks from a table
where I would later plan the thing that ended me.
Same table. Same pen, probably.
I financed my brothers' futures
from the same wood where I financed my own past.
If that's not a punchline I don't know what is.
I just can't find anyone to laugh.

The older one picked up the check with careful hands.
The younger one put his in his pocket and said nothing.
They ate like young men eat. Ambition, no ceremony.
I watched them. I loved them. I still do.
That's not the problem. That was never the problem.
The problem is that love was the only thing on the table
that nobody slid back.
