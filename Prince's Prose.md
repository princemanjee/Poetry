## Loss, Addiction, Recovery

### The Undertow of Grace

I held your chin above the cresting foam,\
And traded all my breath to see you home.\
My shoulders burned beneath the heavy weight,\
While you clawed upward, panicked by your fate.\
I was the raft, the anchor, and the shore,\
Giving you everything, and giving more.

But when your boots at last had struck the sand,\
You did not turn to offer me a hand.\
You shook the water from your heavy coat,\
And walked away, because you were afloat.\
You never looked behind to see the cost,\
Or mark the water where a man was lost.

No grace was given for the life I saved,\
Just silent ripples on an empty grave.\
The current pulled me down into the deep,\
And kept the gratitude you chose to keep.\
I taught your lungs to breathe the open air,\
While mine filled up with what you left me there.

### Day One

The calendar was free. That's how it starts.\
A real estate agency, a stack of neighborhoods,\
April in Wicker Park, all blossoms and no rent,\
tacked to a kitchen wall by someone kind\
who fed the dogs and never asked me why.

I found a pen. Not a good pen. A pen.\
The kind that comes in tens and dies in threes.\
I wrote the number on the day and stood\
and looked at it, and it looked back, and neither\
of us blinked. It was a one. It was a *one.*

No candle. No committee. No applause.\
Nobody sang. The dogs did not convene.\
The building didn't buzz. The lake stayed put.\
Chicago, to its credit, did not care,\
which is the nicest thing Chicago does.

They tell you one's the loneliest. They're right.\
But one is also one more than the zero\
I'd been carrying around like a receipt\
for something I could not remember buying\
and could not, for the life of me, return.

So here's the whole ceremony. Pen. Mark. Down.\
A man, a wall, a free calendar, a dog\
who's on a chair she is not allowed to be on\
and I don't fix it, because who am I\
to lecture Hope about where she belongs.

Tomorrow I will do it all again.\
That's the trick. That's the entire trick.\
It doesn't build. It doesn't crescendo.\
It's the same small mark in the same cheap ink,\
and the ink, thank God, is cheap enough to last.

### Have You Eaten?

Not *hello.* Not *hey.* Never once *how are you.*\
The question came before the phone was warm:\
*Have you eaten?* And I'd lie, because the truth\
was peanut butter, bread, a knife, no plate,\
a meal that only bachelors and raccoons\
would classify as dinner. *I'm about to.*\
*About to what.* *Eat.* *Eat what.* *A sandwich.*\
*A sandwich is a cry for help,* she'd say,\
and I could hear the corners of her mouth\
go round. You can. You can hear a smile.\
I catalogued it. I catalogued everything.

The question was a lock she checked each night.\
You don't answer it. You just keep the door shut.\
*Move the boxes.* *Tonight.* *Tonight meaning tonight.*\
*Not tonight meaning Saturday.* *Tonight.*\
And keys, and a door, and the rustle of a woman\
with somewhere to be and a husband to supervise\
in the basic arts of protein and of Christmas.

I still stand in kitchens.\
I still hold the knife.\
The bread still sits there like it's owed a plate.\
And every phone I've owned since then has rung\
and not one of them has asked me what I've had.

You want to know what loss is? It's not the funeral.\
It's not the flowers or the folded program\
or a room of people saying *if you need anything.*\
It's a jar of Jif at ten o'clock at night\
and nobody on earth to tell you *that's not food.*

I ate the sandwich. Eventually. Years later.\
It had protein. So does dog food. I know.\
I know. I know. I know. I can still hear you.

### The Floor

They said grief is a room. Go in. Sit down.\
Cry on the furniture. Come out when done.\
That's what the pamphlet said, or would have said,\
if I had ever let one in the house.

Mine wasn't a room.\
Mine was the floor.

Every enterprise I ever built,\
the lemonade, the limos, the degrees,\
the dinners, the careers, the second wife,\
the third wife, the operation, the rescues, the cell,\
the sobriety, the dogs, the plywood counter,\
all of it sat on the same slab of poured cement\
and the cement was a boy at a grave in Indiana\
who had a name that meant one thing\
beside a stone that meant the same thing, feminine.

You don't feel a foundation. That's the point.\
You walk on it. You put a couch on it.\
You get a mortgage. You forget it's there.\
And then one day you're sitting with a dog\
and something in your breathing shifts, and the dog\
notices before you do, because the dog\
is calibrated like a seismograph to the tremors\
of the large sad animal it lives with,\
and you look down and think:\
*oh. That's what I've been standing on.*

Every horse with ribs like an architect's sketch.\
Every dog four days from the needle.\
Every family learning what January does.\
Same face. Same face. Same face.\
A debt that isn't a number.\
A debt that is a name.

I tried to pay it off in animals.\
Turns out the interest is compounding\
and the bank is a fourteen-year-old boy\
who won't take cash, or horses, or apologies,\
and won't say what he wants, because he's fourteen,\
and because he's me.

### The Twenty-Mile Rule

I had a rule. I want you to appreciate\
the elegance of it. Twenty miles. At all times.\
Twenty miles from the product, from the cash,\
from the warehouse and the hands that touched the thing.\
I managed from a distance. I was good at it.\
Distance was the one thing I could build\
faster than a lemonade stand at eight years old.

Twenty miles is, in Chicago, forty minutes.\
In Indiana it's one bad decision and a cornfield.\
On the map it's a circle. In the body it's nothing.\
The body doesn't do miles. The body does *yes.*

Here's the joke, and it's a good one, hold on:\
I built a ring around the poison\
and stood in the middle of it\
and called the middle *clean.*\
Twenty miles from the drugs.\
Zero miles from the reason.
